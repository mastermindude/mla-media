What the Formula Misses

A registry can count people.
It cannot define them.

The cut-off works mechanically. Families do not.

Identity does not obey a registry

The cut-off operates mechanically. It does not examine a person’s ancestry, family circumstances, or relationship with a First Nation (Assembly of First Nations).

Relatives can receive different legal answers

People from the same family can be placed in different registration categories even when their ancestry and community relationships are shared.

The label has practical consequences

Registration can affect access to rights, benefits, services, and programs connected to status (Amnesty International Canada).

The paperwork changes. Their relationship does not.

Why the myth survives

The rule is mostly invisible until it is not.

Federal registration is often confused with Indigenous identity. The difference between 6(1) and 6(2) is invisible in ordinary conversation. The cut-off may remain hidden until a grandchild is born.

Myths about automatic benefits also make Status sound like a permanent prize instead of a restrictive legal category.

The paperwork is quiet. Its consequences are not.

A question beyond the formula

Who gets to decide belonging?

Indian Status is a federal registration system. Indigenous identity, citizenship, and community belonging are broader questions that cannot be reduced to a card or category.
