A register.
Not an identity.

Indian Status is federal registration under the Indian Act. It recognizes certain First Nations people for specific legal rights, programs, and services.

It is not a government-issued measurement of culture, ancestry, or belonging. (Indigenous Services Canada)

  • Many First Nations people are non-status.
  • Inuit and Métis are not registered under the Indian Act.
  • A status card cannot measure community belonging.

The word “Indian” appears here because it remains the legal term used by the Indian Act and in the name “Indian Status.”

Legal entitlement
can end inside a
living family tree.

Kim Wheeler explains that a registered parent may have a registered child, while the next generation may not qualify if that child later has a child with someone who is not entitled to registration. (Wheeler)

How the rule works

Same rights.
Different transmission.

People registered under 6(1) and 6(2) can access the same status-linked rights, programs, and services. Their ability to pass on entitlement is different. (Indigenous Services Canada)

6(1)

Can transmit entitlement when the other parent is not entitled.

6(2)

Can transmit entitlement only when the other parent is also entitled.

Test the rule.

Change a parent.

This model isolates the central transmission rule. The complete registration system contains additional categories and exceptions. (Indigenous Services Canada)

Three generations.

After two consecutive generations of parenting with someone who is not entitled, the third generation may no longer qualify for registration. (Indigenous Services Canada)

What the formula
misses.

Identity does not obey a registry.

The cut-off operates mechanically. It does not examine a person’s ancestry, family circumstances, or relationship with a First Nation. (Assembly of First Nations)

Relatives can receive different legal answers.

People from the same family can be placed in different registration categories even when their ancestry and community relationships are shared. (Indigenous Services Canada)

The label has practical consequences.

Registration may affect access to reserve lands, treaty rights, health services, education funding, and other programs. (Amnesty International Canada)

Why the myth
survives.

  • Federal registration is often confused with Indigenous identity.
  • The difference between 6(1) and 6(2) is invisible in ordinary conversation.
  • The cut-off may remain hidden until a grandchild is born.
  • Myths about automatic benefits make Status sound like a permanent prize instead of a restrictive legal category.

The law now.

As of 14 October 2026. Proposed changes are not law.

1985The structure begins

Bill C-31 introduced the general 6(1) and 6(2) registration structure associated with the second-generation cut-off. (Indigenous Services Canada)

December 2025A proposed change

The Senate amended Bill S-2 to include the removal of the second-generation cut-off and a proposed one-parent rule. (Parliament of Canada)

14 October 2026Not yet law

Bill S-2 remains under consideration by the Standing Committee on Indigenous and Northern Affairs in the House of Commons. (Parliament of Canada)

Why should a distant federal system decide who belongs to a First Nation?

Identity outlives

The status system can shrink a legal population without changing the people. A federal register may decide whom Canada recognizes. It cannot make a child less connected to family, community, or Nation.
